Background &
Problem Statement




                                3
BACKGROUND
The Toronto Transit Commission (TTC) is Toronto’s primary public transit
provider, connecting millions of journeys across the city. However, aging
infrastructure, traffic congestion, and funding shortfalls continue to challenge
the system’s reliability and capacity (Montagnese, 2026). In 2024, subway
reduced-speed zones peaked at nearly three dozen, while approximately $37
billion in the TTC’s long-term capital investment needs remain unfunded (Lali,
2026; Swadden, 2025). These pressures underscore the need for strategic
investments that improve mobility while making effective use of limited
resources.


Other Canadian transit systems demonstrate how infrastructure and
operational choices can influence performance. Vancouver’s SkyTrain uses
automated, grade-separated trains to provide frequent and reliable service
(Steer Davies Gleave, 2012). Meanwhile, Waterloo Region’s ION light rail transit
(LRT) achieved 95.3% on-time performance in 2025 (Region of Waterloo
Transportation Services, 2025). Transit signal priority, which reduces delays at
intersections, is among the operational measures supporting efficient light rail
service (Kalimipalli et al., 2026). Although these systems operate under
different geographic and demographic conditions, they illustrate how distinct
technologies and operating strategies can address transportation challenges.




                                                                            4
BACKGROUND (cont.)
Recognizing the need for strategic expansion, the City of Toronto evaluated 24
potential higher-order transit corridors in 2024. Its Rapid Transit Evaluation
Framework assessed corridors according to three guiding principles: Serve
People, Strengthen Places, and Support Prosperity (City of Toronto, 2024). The
evaluation incorporated measures of ridership, accessibility, travel-time
savings, and broader community benefits.


Determining where to invest, however, involves competing priorities.
Statistical analysis, mathematical modelling, and computational optimization
offer systematic approaches to evaluating these trade-offs, identifying
promising corridors, and determining which transit technologies can deliver
the greatest benefits.




                                                                             5
Problem Statement:
In January 2026, the Toronto Transit Commission (TTC) proposed a $16.657
billion capital budget and plan for 2026–2035, largely dedicated to maintaining,
modernizing, and improving Toronto’s existing transit infrastructure (Toronto
Transit Commission, 2026).


In an alternative scenario, the City of Toronto has instead reallocated $16.657
billion of this funding exclusively toward constructing one new rapid-transit
corridor. The fictional Lead Agency for Rapid-transit Planning, Expansion, and
Rider Services (LARPERS) has been entrusted with delivering the project and
has contracted your team as independent mathematical and computational
consultants.


Drawing from the 24 corridors identified in Toronto’s 2024 Prioritization of
Higher-Order Transit: Corridor Evaluation Procedure, your team must develop
two sequential mathematical models. The first must identify the strongest
corridor through exploratory statistical analysis and mathematical evaluation,
independently of construction costs. The second must determine the most
appropriate     transit   technology   through   computational,   multi-criteria
optimization.


The recommended project must remain within the $16.657 billion capital
ceiling and be fully operational by December 31, 2035. Assume favourable
political and economic conditions, without extraordinary administrative delays,
funding interruptions, or unexpected inflationary shocks. Realistic engineering
and construction constraints still apply.


The question posed to you now is then: where should Toronto invest, what
should it build, and how can mathematics justify that decision?

The Challenge




                            7
The challenge
The Lead Agency for Rapid-transit Planning, Expansion, and Rider Services
(LARPERS) requires your team to recommend one rapid-transit corridor and
one technology for its proposed investment. Your recommendation must be
supported by two sequential mathematical models, developed using the City of
Toronto’s 2024 corridor evaluation report and any additional publicly accessible
evidence.


 *You may access this report here:
 https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2024/ex/bgrd/backgroundfile-243172.pdf

 (This is the only data set that will be provided by UTSRI. You are responsible for sourcing
 your own data from publicly available sources.)



To assist you and your team to determine the best possible recommendation,
you are required to follow the two-part outline for this challenge, explained
below.



01 Where Should Toronto Invest?
Develop a mathematical model to identify the strongest corridor among the 24
candidates in the document, independently of construction costs and budget
considerations. Your analysis must address the following:



1
    Exploratory Statistical Analysis: Examine the corridor data using descriptive
    statistics, relationships between relevant variables, and at least one
    statistical visualization. Explain how your findings inform your model.



2   Variable Selection: Identify and justify the variables used to evaluate the
    corridors. You may select, exclude, transform, or supplement the City's
    evaluation measures, provided your choices are mathematically and
    contextually justified.


                                                                                               8
The challenge (cont.)
01 Where Should Toronto Invest?

3   Mathematical Evaluation: Construct a quantitative model that evaluates all
    24 corridors using your selected variables. Clearly explain your
    mathematical approach, assumptions, and interpretation of the results. For
    example, an illustrative matrix-based evaluation approach is provided in
    Appendix A for your reference. Teams are not required to adopt this approach.



4   Corridor Recommendation: Identify the highest-priority corridor and justify
    its selection using your model's findings.



 Important:
 Construction costs, budget constraints, and cost-normalized evaluation measures
 must not influence corridor selection. The chosen corridor will remain fixed
 throughout Part 2, regardless of subsequent technology or cost considerations.




02 What Should Toronto Build?
Develop and computationally implement a multi-criteria optimization model
to determine the most suitable rapid-transit technology for your selected
corridor. Your analysis must address the following:



1
    Technology Comparison: Evaluate at least two distinct rapid-transit
    technologies. Bus rapid transit (BRT), light rail transit (LRT), and subway are
    possible options, but other defensible technologies may be considered.



2   Optimization Model: Develop a mathematical objective function
    incorporating, at minimum: (1) capital cost-effectiveness, (2) projected
    ridership, and (3) travel-time savings. You may introduce additional criteria.
    Assign and justify numerical weights, ensuring that overlapping criteria are
    appropriately addressed. For example, an illustrative weighted multi-criteria
    optimization model is provided in Appendix B for your reference. Teams are
    not required to adopt this approach.
                                                                                   9
The challenge (cont.)
02 What Should Toronto Build?

3   Computational Implementation: Implement your optimization model using
    R, Python, or another suitable programming language. Present the resulting
    technology evaluations and identify the preferred feasible alternative.



4   Sensitivity Analysis: Computationally investigate how changes in criterion
    weights affect your recommendation. Demonstrate whether the preferred
    technology remains consistent under alternative priorities and, where
    applicable, identify conditions under which another technology becomes
    preferable.



5   Implementation Feasibility: Provide one evidence-based capital cost
    estimate for each evaluated technology and a credible development timeline
    for the recommended project. The selected technology must satisfy the
    $16.657 billion capital ceiling and be fully constructed, tested, and
    operational by December 31, 2035.



03 Mathematical and Statistical Expectations

Teams must develop mathematically rigorous, evidence-based models that
meaningfully support their recommendations. They are free to select
appropriate mathematical, statistical, and computational techniques, provided
their methods are clearly explained and justified.

Part 1 does not require programming, while Part 2 requires both
computational implementation and weight sensitivity analysis. Independent
ridership forecasting, formal hypothesis testing, and quantitative
construction scheduling are not required.

The challenge (cont.)
04 Project Constraints
The following conditions apply to both parts of the challenge:
    Select exactly one of the 24 eligible corridors, retaining its original alignment
    and endpoints. Minor in-line or terminus stop adjustments are permitted.
    Recommend one technology for the entire corridor; mixed-mode designs
    are not permitted.
    Use publicly accessible, appropriately cited data and evidence-based
    estimates. The City's original technology assumptions are not binding.
    Apply the $16.657 billion ceiling to capital costs only; operating and lifecycle
    costs are optional, and consultant costs are excluded.
    Assume favourable political and economic conditions, while accounting for
    realistic engineering and construction requirements.
Your final presentation must explain models in both parts of the challenge,
defend your recommendation, and briefly discuss relevant assumptions,
limitations, potential biases, and data weaknesses.

Supplementary Submission
Requirements
In addition to the deliverables outlined in the general competition guidelines,
teams must submit a ZIP archive containing the datasets, computational files,
and supporting documentation used in Parts 1 and 2, with the following:



1   Part 1: Corridor Evaluation Dataset
    A structured dataset containing all 24 eligible corridors and the variables
    used in the team's exploratory statistical analysis and mathematical
    evaluation. Teams must independently extract, select, clean, and organize
    the relevant data from the City's 2024 corridor evaluation report and any
    supplementary public sources. All transformations and derived variables
    must be documented. Suggestion: This dataset is where you base your
    descriptive statistics from.



2   Part 2: Technology Comparison Dataset
    A separate dataset containing the performance estimates and evaluation
    criteria for at least two transit technologies applied to the selected corridor.
    Teams must construct this dataset using publicly accessible information,
    including relevant transit studies, agency reports, and comparable projects.
    Evidence-based calculations and extrapolations are permitted when
    appropriately justified.



3   Computational Source Code
    Executable source code implementing Part 2's optimization model and
    sensitivity analysis, using R, Python, or another suitable programming
    language. Source code for Part 1 is optional.



4   Computational Outputs
    Relevant tables, figures, and results generated by the models, including the
    final corridor evaluation, technology comparison, and sensitivity analysis.



5   Documentation (README.md)
    A document identifying the original data sources, explaining data
    preparation and transformations, listing software dependencies, and
    providing instructions for reproducing the computational results. 13

 Data Transparency
All datasets must be submitted in accessible formats, such as CSV or XLSX, with
clearly labelled variables and units. Teams must disclose their sources,
assumptions, and calculations sufficiently for judges to trace and evaluate the
data used.

Teams will be assessed on the relevance, validity, and transparency of their
datasets, not their size or complexity. Unsupported or fabricated data are not
permitted.

Appendix A: Illustrative Matrix-Based Corridor Evaluation

One possible approach to Part 1 is a matrix-based multi-criteria evaluation, comparing the 24 candidate
corridors across k selected criteria.
Let X be a 24 × k matrix of standardized criterion values:
                                                  ⎡                        ⎤
                                           x11                 ···     x1k
                                          ⎢ ..                 ..       .. ⎥
                                        X=⎣ .                     .      . ⎦
                                                   x24,1       ···     x24,k

Each numerical criterion may be standardized using:

                                                           aij − āj
                                                  xij =
                                                              sj

where aij is the original value, āj is the criterion mean, and sj is its nonzero sample standard deviation.
Criteria should be oriented so that higher values indicate more desirable outcomes.
Assign a weight wj to each criterion, subject to:

                                             k
                                            ∑︂
                                                  wj = 1,             wj ≥ 0
                                            j=1

The weighted corridor scores are calculated through matrix multiplication:


                                                      s = Xw

The highest-scoring corridor is selected:


                                             i∗ = arg          max       si
                                                          i∈{1,...,24}


Teams should justify their variables, transformations, and weights using their exploratory statistical
findings. Construction costs and cost-dependent measures must not influence Part 1.

Appendix B: Illustrative Multi-Criteria Technology Optimization

One possible approach to Part 2 is a weighted multi-criteria optimization model comparing transit
technologies for the corridor selected in Part 1.
Let m ∈ M denote a candidate technology. Define:
 • E(m): normalized capital cost-effectiveness;
 • R(m): normalized projected ridership;
 • T (m): normalized travel-time savings;
 • C(m): estimated capital cost;
 • wE , wR , wT : corresponding weights.
The objective is to maximize the combined performance score:


                             max U (m) = wE E(m) + wR R(m) + wT T (m)
                             m∈M


subject to the weighting constraints:


                                          wE + w R + w T = 1


                                           wE , wR , wT ≥ 0

and the capital budget constraint:


                                          C(m) ≤ 16.6 × 109

The recommended technology is therefore:


                                        m∗ =      arg max      U (m)
                                                   m∈M
                                               C(m)≤16.6×109


Teams must justify their criteria, weights, normalization methods, and performance estimates. Potential
overlap between criteria, such as ridership and cost-effectiveness, should be appropriately addressed.
Computational sensitivity analysis must examine how changes in criterion weights affect the preferred
technology. The recommended project must also be operational by December 31, 2035.


Important Note. These formulations are illustrative, not prescriptive. Teams may develop alternative
mathematically justified models.
